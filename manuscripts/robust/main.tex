\title{Random-walk level noise breaks anytime-valid trend monitoring:\\ a failure theorem, a universal e-process and the price of robustness}
\author{Author information removed for anonymous peer review}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Anytime-valid monitoring of a trend usually assumes that the noise around a smooth path is independent. We show that this fails under the smallest departure from it. If the latent level carries a random-walk disturbance of any positive variance, every fixed-shape contrast statistic has variance inflated by a factor that grows as the square of the series length, so that e-processes built on independent noise declare a change with probability tending to one; we give the exact rate and the sample size at which the failure begins. We then construct a universal e-process whose null class contains the random-walk level with unknown variance, an unknown scale of the noise, unknown regime levels and a linear trend, and prove that it is valid for every member of an arbitrary parametric Gaussian covariance class, whatever the mixture over alternatives. A decomposition separates the cost of robustness into the mixture over alternatives, the supremum over nuisance parameters and the information destroyed by the roughness itself, and a spike-in calibration gives the minimum detectable change. The motivating application is the Business Trends and Outlook Survey of the U.S.\ Census Bureau (56 waves on business use of artificial intelligence): the roughness is highly significant, an independence-based monitor declares changes in almost every series simulated from the fitted null, and the universal e-process finds no evidence of a change in growth, with a detectable change that is three to four times the average growth rate.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} anytime-valid inference; e-process; change detection; random-walk noise; spurious trend; survey time series.

\section{Introduction}

Users of a sequence of published estimates inspect each release and ask whether the growth of the indicator has changed. Testing at every release inflates error rates \citep{armitage1969}, and the modern remedy is anytime-valid inference, in which e-values and test martingales control the error uniformly over time \citep{howard2021,vovk2021,ramdas2023,grunwald2024}. Constructions for change detection and sequential experiments take the observations to be, given the past, draws from a fixed parametric family with independent noise \citep{johari2022,shin2023,shin2024}. For a smooth trend this means independent errors around a deterministic path, and the e-process monitors departures of the path.

\paragraph{The problem.} Independent noise around a straight line is rarely credible for a published series. The latent level moves from wave to wave for reasons unrelated to growth, and a random walk with small variance is the simplest description \citep{harvey1989,harvey2000,durbin2012}. In the fixed-sample trend-testing literature a persistent noise component is known to invalidate tests that assume independence, and it is the reason for spurious regressions \citep{granger1974,phillips1986} and for the robust trend tests of \citet{vogelsang1998}, \citet{bunzel2005}, \citet{harvey2007} and \citet{perron2009}. Those results do not transfer to the sequential setting. A fixed-sample test is wrong at one sample size; a monitor that is inspected at every release and uses a threshold that grows only logarithmically is wrong in a way that worsens with every release, and its error is not controlled by any rule that ignores the dependence. Nothing in the anytime-valid literature quantifies this, and none of its constructions has a null class that contains a rough level.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A failure theorem} (Section \ref{sec:failure}). For any fixed-shape contrast of an equally spaced series, a random-walk level of variance $p$ per wave and sampling variance $\sigma^2$ inflate the variance of the standardized statistic to $1 + (p/\sigma^2)\,c\,n^2\,(1+o(1))$ with a constant $c>0$ that depends on the contrast only (for curvature $c = 1/42$). The independence-based e-process therefore declares with probability tending to one, for every $p>0$. The inflation is already 150-fold at the 54 waves of our application, and the failure begins when $n \approx \sigma/\sqrt{pc}$, about five waves for the curvature of the series studied here. The contrast form shows that the failure is not specific to the curvature statistic: it covers slope-change and level-shift contrasts.
\item \emph{A universal e-process and its validity theorem} (Section \ref{sec:method}). For a Gaussian null class whose covariance may be any consistent parametric family, we give an e-process that is the ratio of an arbitrary mixture over alternatives to the supremum of the invariant (restricted) likelihood of the null, and prove it valid for every member of the class (Theorem \ref{thm:validity}). The random-walk level, the first-order autoregressive level and a random slope are special cases. The class is correlated and has nuisance parameters in the covariance, which the existing universal-inference and invariance arguments do not cover.
\item \emph{The price of robustness} (Sections \ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:application}). An exact inequality separates the loss into the mixture, the supremum and the information that the roughness destroys, a simulated decomposition shows that most of the loss is the mixture, and a spike-in calibration on the design of the application gives the minimum detectable change. On the 56 waves of the Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) on business use of artificial intelligence, the series could reveal a change of slope of 0.43 points per wave with probability 50 percent and 0.51 with probability 80 percent, against an average growth of 0.12.
\end{enumerate}
The empirical finding is a null result and it is stated as one. The paper does not claim that the growth of business use of artificial intelligence is constant; it shows that this series cannot reveal a change smaller than three to four times its average growth once its roughness is accounted for, and that the changes that an independence-based monitor reports are produced by the roughness.

\paragraph{Related work.} The e-process machinery we use is standard: Ville's inequality, mixture martingales \citep{robbins1970}, invariance with respect to a location nuisance \citep{perezortiz2024} and the composite null construction of universal inference \citep{wasserman2020,ruf2023}. Sequential change detection is typically built on cumulative-sum or generalized likelihood-ratio statistics with calibrated thresholds \citep{page1954,siegmund1995,lorden2005}; e-detectors \citep{shin2024} give anytime-valid versions for independent observations. We do not propose a new mixture or a new detector. The contribution is the failure result, which has no counterpart in the anytime-valid literature, the validity theorem for a correlated null class, and the quantified cost. State-space models for survey series with a rough level \citep{scott1974,durbin2012,harvey2000} estimate the trend and level; they do not give sequential error control, and their likelihood-ratio tests for roughness have non-standard null distributions \citep{self1987}.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

Let $y_1, \dots, y_n$ be published estimates at release times $t_1 < \dots < t_n$ (in units of the release interval) with published design-based standard errors $s_j$, and let $\rho_j$ label the regime (here, the questionnaire wording). Write $T_j = t_j - t_1$, $D = \mathrm{diag}(s_1^2, \dots, s_n^2)$ and $W_{jk} = \min(T_j, T_k)$. The null class is
\begin{equation}
y \sim \mathcal{N}\big(X c,\ \kappa^2 (D + r\,W)\big), \qquad c \in \mathbb{R}^{q},\ \kappa^2 > 0,\ r \in \mathcal{R}_0,
\label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
where $X$ has one column per regime (an unknown level for each) and one column $t_j$ (an unknown common slope). Equation \eqref{eq:null} is the marginal law of $y_j = L_j + e_j$ with sampling error $e_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \kappa^2 s_j^2)$ and level $L_j = L_1 + b\,T_j + \sum_{i \le j} \eta_i$, $\eta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \kappa^2 r\,(T_i - T_{i-1}))$: a linear trend plus a random walk. The roughness $r \ge 0$ is measured relative to $\kappa^2$, and $p = \kappa^2 r$ is the variance of the level disturbance per wave. The roughness is a statistical description of the departures of the level from a line; we do not attribute it to a cause, and it is not identified against sampling error that is correlated across waves (for example through panel overlap), which the published standard errors, treating waves as independent, would understate. The set $\mathcal{R}_0$ is a finite grid that contains zero; we use 46 values spanning four orders of magnitude. The covariance of the first $n$ waves is the leading block of that of the first $n+1$, so \eqref{eq:null} defines a family of laws on the sequence.

Two families of alternatives are monitored, each at an unknown wave $k$ and with an unknown size $\delta$: a change of slope, $\E y = Xc + \delta\,(t - t_k)_+$, and a level shift, $\E y = Xc + \delta\,\mathbf{1}\{j \ge k\}$. A level shift of this kind is what an unannounced instrument event looks like. Both the horizon $n_{\max}$ and all grids and prior scales used below are fixed before the data are seen; the application is retrospective.

\section{Independence-based monitors fail under level roughness}
\label{sec:failure}

\paragraph{A contrast statistic.} Take an equally spaced series, $t_j = j$, with $s_j = \sigma$. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a contrast orthogonal to the null design, $X^\top a = 0$ (for this section, $X = (1, j)$), and
\[
Z_a = \frac{a^\top y}{\sigma\|a\|},
\]
which is $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ under independence. Curvature, a slope change at a fixed wave and a level shift at a fixed wave are all of this form after the null design is projected out. The independence-based monitor with prior scale $\tau$ declares when the mixture e-process $E_n(a) = (1+\tau^2 B)^{-1/2}\exp\{\tau^2 A^2/(2(1+\tau^2 B))\}$, with $A = a^\top y/\sigma^2$ and $B = \|a\|^2/\sigma^2$, reaches $2/\alpha$ \citep{robbins1970}; the variant with a half-normal prior behaves identically for our purposes and is the one used in the simulations.

\begin{theorem}[failure]
\label{thm:failure}
Let $y_j = \beta_0 + \beta_1 j + L_j + e_j$ with $e_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ independent and $L$ a Gaussian random walk with increment variance $p>0$. Let $a = a^{(n)}$ be the residual of $\psi(j/n)$ on $(1,j)$, where $\psi$ is piecewise continuous on $[0,1]$ and is not almost everywhere equal to a linear function. Put $\tilde\psi$ for the residual of $\psi$ on $(1,x)$ in $L_2[0,1]$ and $\Psi(x) = \int_x^1 \tilde\psi$. Then
\[
\mathrm{Var}(Z_a) = 1 + \lambda_n,\qquad \lambda_n = \frac{p}{\sigma^2}\,\frac{a^\top W a}{\|a\|^2} = \frac{p}{\sigma^2}\,c(\psi)\,n^2\,(1 + o(1)),\qquad c(\psi) = \frac{\int_0^1 \Psi^2}{\int_0^1\tilde\psi^2} > 0 .
\]
Consequently, for every $\tau>0$, $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and $p>0$, the probability that $E_n(a)\ge 2/\alpha$ tends to one as $n\to\infty$. For curvature, $\psi(x) = x^2$ and $c = 1/42$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is in Appendix \ref{app:proof}. The quantity $\lambda_n$ is the Rayleigh quotient of $W$ against the sampling covariance on the contrast, and it is exact for any $a$; the asymptotic form gives the rate. Three consequences follow. First, the phase transition: the inflation exceeds one when $n > n^\ast = \sigma/\sqrt{p\,c}$; for the curvature of the BTOS design, with $r/s^2 \approx 2$, this is about five waves. Second, the statement is not an artefact of the half-normal mixture or of known scale; Proposition \ref{prop:scale} treats estimation of the scale. Third, the failure is in the variance, not in a bias: the statistic is centred and a simple recalibration of its threshold would restore the level at one $n$ but not uniformly over $n$, since the inflation grows.

\begin{proposition}[unknown scale; proof sketch]
\label{prop:scale}
For curvature, the same conclusion holds for the version of the e-process in which $\sigma^2$ is estimated from the residual sum of squares of the fit of $(1,j)$: the studentized statistic diverges at rate $\sqrt{n}$.
\end{proposition}

\section{A universal e-process}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Construction and validity}

Let $X_n$ be the design of the first $n$ waves and $R_n$ the data modulo the column space of $X_n$. Under \eqref{eq:null} the law of $R_n$ does not depend on $c$, and it has a density with respect to a fixed reference measure on the residual space, of the restricted likelihood (REML) form
\[
P_\theta(R_n) \propto |\Sigma_\theta|^{-1/2}\,|X_n^\top \Sigma_\theta^{-1} X_n|^{-1/2}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\, y^\top P_\theta\, y\big), \qquad \Sigma_\theta = \kappa^2(D + rW),
\]
with $P_\theta = \Sigma_\theta^{-1} - \Sigma_\theta^{-1}X_n (X_n^\top\Sigma_\theta^{-1}X_n)^{-1}X_n^\top\Sigma_\theta^{-1}$. A component $c = (g, f, k, \tau)$ of the alternative has a nuisance setting $g = (r_g, \kappa_g^2)$, a family $f$ (slope or level), a change wave $k$ and a prior scale $\tau$ for $\delta$. With $z$ the alternative column, $a = z^\top P_g y$ and $b = z^\top P_g z$ (with $P_g$ computed at $\kappa^2=1$ scaled by $\kappa_g^{-2}$),
\[
Q_{c}(R_n) = P_{g}(R_n)\cdot \mathrm{BF}_c, \qquad \mathrm{BF}_c = (1 + \tau^2 b)^{-1/2}\exp\!\Big(\frac{\tau^2 a^2}{2(1 + \tau^2 b)}\Big),
\]
the density of $R_n$ when $\delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2)$. Let $Q_n = \sum_c w_c Q_c$ with fixed weights and
\begin{equation}
E_n = \frac{Q_n(R_n)}{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} P_\theta(R_n)}, \qquad \Theta_0 = \{(\kappa^2, r): \kappa^2 > 0,\ r \in \mathcal{R}_0\}.
\label{eq:E}
\end{equation}
The supremum over $\kappa^2$ is explicit, $\hat\kappa^2 = y^\top P_{1,r}\,y/\nu$ with $\nu = n-q$, and the supremum over $r$ is a search over the grid. The weights are uniform over the two families, the change waves $k\in\{3,\dots,n_{\max}\}$, five roughness values from zero to 32 times the median sampling variance, four values of $\kappa_g^2$ from 0.5 to 4, and four prior scales per family (slope: 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8; level: 0.5, 1, 2, 4), about 8,300 components, a log-weight of 9 nats. Components whose change wave lies beyond the current wave have $z=0$ on the data so far and contribute the null density.

\begin{theorem}[validity]
\label{thm:validity}
Let $\{\Sigma_\theta(n)\}_{\theta\in\Theta_0}$ be any collection of positive-definite covariance matrices, consistent in $n$ (each $\Sigma_\theta(n)$ is the leading block of $\Sigma_\theta(n+1)$), and $X_n$ designs for which the column space of $X_{n+1}$ restricted to the first $n$ rows is that of $X_n$. Let $Q_n$ be any mixture of consistent Gaussian laws of $R_n$ with positive-definite covariance, and $E_n$ as in \eqref{eq:E}. For every $\theta\in\Theta_0$ and $c$, if $y\sim\mathcal{N}(X c,\Sigma_\theta)$ then $\Prob_\theta(\exists n: E_n\ge 1/\alpha)\le\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
Theorem \ref{thm:validity} covers the random-walk level of \eqref{eq:null}, a first-order autoregressive level with unknown correlation, a random slope (integrated random walk), and any finite or countable union of such classes.
\end{corollary}

The proof (Appendix \ref{app:proof}) shows that $\Lambda_n = Q_n/P_\theta$ is a likelihood-ratio martingale and that $E_n\le\Lambda_n$ because the supremum is at least $P_\theta$; Ville's inequality then gives the claim. The step that is not in the cited arguments is the first: the family of residual laws is consistent and the residual $\sigma$-fields are nested for a correlated null with nuisance parameters in the covariance, which is why the restricted likelihood, and not the likelihood, is the object. Nothing depends on how $Q_n$ is mixed, so the construction is a template. The grid for $\mathcal{R}_0$ can be replaced by the continuum with the supremum in \eqref{eq:E}; validity holds for any set and the only requirement is that the supremum be attained or approximated from above by a measurable function.

\subsection{The price of robustness}

\begin{proposition}[decomposition]
\label{prop:cost}
For every component $c$ with nuisance setting $g\in\Theta_0$,
\begin{equation}
\log E_n \ \ge\ \underbrace{\log w_c}_{\text{mixture}} + \underbrace{\log \Lambda_{c,n}}_{\text{known-}\theta\text{ LR}} - \underbrace{\Delta_n}_{\text{supremum}}, \qquad \Delta_n = \log \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} P_\theta(R_n) - \log P_{g}(R_n) \ \ge 0,
\label{eq:oracle}
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda_{c,n} = Q_c/P_g$ is the likelihood ratio of a user who knows the nuisance setting $g$ and the component.
\end{proposition}

The inequality is an identity-level bound and is loose in two ways. The mixture costs $\log(1/w_c)$, which is about 9 nats here. The supremum costs $\Delta_n$, which under the null at $g$ is a likelihood-ratio statistic for two parameters and is $O_P(1)$ for interior $g$; under an alternative the null fit can absorb signal, and $\Delta_n$ is not controlled by the inequality. Its size is therefore measured, not bounded. The first term of the known-$\theta$ benchmark grows linearly in information: for a simple alternative with slope change $\delta$ at wave $k$, $\E\log\Lambda = \tfrac12\delta^2 b_k(\Sigma)$ with $b_k(\Sigma) = z_k^\top P\, z_k$, which is what roughness destroys. The information is a scale, not a detection threshold, and we calibrate detection by simulation rather than by a formula.

\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}

All simulations use the dates and published standard errors of the 54 legacy-wording BTOS waves, a linear mean of slope 0.12 points per wave, level $\alpha = 0.05$ (threshold 20 for a single process) and $n_{\max} = 54$.

\paragraph{Validity.} The probability that the universal e-process ever reaches 20 is 0.000 for independent errors, 0.000 for standard errors 1.5 times the published ones, and 0.000, 0.001 and 0.000 for random-walk levels with $p=0.03$, 0.06 and 0.2 per wave (1,000 replications each), against 1.00 for the independence-based monitor at $p=0.06$. For autoregressive levels with correlation 0.9 and 0.98, which lie outside the random-walk class, the universal e-process is also never above the threshold, and enlarging the class with an autoregressive-level setting changes the power at $p=0.06$, slope 0.4 from 0.41 to 0.39 with no loss of validity. These probabilities are far below $\alpha$: the construction is conservative, and Table \ref{tab:power} shows the price.

\paragraph{Where the cost comes from.} Table \ref{tab:oracle} separates the three terms of \eqref{eq:oracle}. The known-$\theta$ benchmark, which knows the nuisance setting, the family, the size and the wave, detects a slope change of 0.4 at $p=0.06$ in 99 percent of replications. Adding the supremum over the null parameters, for a single component, reduces this to 87 percent. The mixture over about 8,300 components reduces it to 40 percent. At smaller changes the supremum also costs a great deal (0.40 to 0.10 at slope 0.2), and the mixture takes the rest. The robustness is not mainly paid for by the supremum: it is paid for by the breadth of the alternatives.

\paragraph{Comparison with a calibrated test.} A user who knew the null class could instead calibrate a likelihood-ratio monitor by simulation. We took the maximum over waves, families and change waves of the generalized likelihood ratio, with maximum-likelihood nuisance parameters, and set the threshold at the 95 percent quantile of its null distribution at the least favourable roughness. Table \ref{tab:glr} shows that this calibrated monitor is no more powerful than the universal e-process in the cases that matter (0.23 against 0.39 for slope 0.4 at $p=0.06$; 0.70 against 0.78 for a level shift of 2), and is more powerful only where neither detects. The e-process buys exact anytime validity with no calibration and costs nothing in power relative to the calibrated alternative in this design.

\section{Application: business use of artificial intelligence}
\label{sec:application}

\paragraph{Data.} The BTOS is the Census Bureau's biweekly survey of about 1.2 million employer businesses \citep{censusmethod2025}. We use the 56 national waves on current use of artificial intelligence from 24 September 2023 to 14 December 2025 \citep{bonney2024}: 54 with the legacy wording and two with the wording introduced on 17 November 2025, which has its own level. The estimate rises from 3.7 to 10.0 percent under the legacy wording; the published standard errors have median 0.15.

\paragraph{Roughness and model choice.} Maximizing the restricted likelihood over \eqref{eq:null} gives $\hat p = 0.058$ points$^2$ per wave (95 percent profile interval 0.018 to 0.46) and $\hat\kappa^2 = 1.28$; the level disturbance has standard deviation 0.24 points per wave against a sampling standard deviation of 0.15. Setting $r=0$ lowers the restricted log-likelihood by 39.8, a likelihood-ratio statistic of 79.6; because $r=0$ is on the boundary, the reference distribution is the mixture $\tfrac12\chi^2_0+\tfrac12\chi^2_1$ \citep{self1987} and the evidence is overwhelming. Table \ref{tab:models} compares models by AIC. A straight line with a random-walk level is best; adding curvature does not improve it, and independent noise is far worse even with a quadratic mean. The data therefore support roughness and do not additionally support curvature.

\paragraph{What an independence-based monitor would say.} The monitor of Section \ref{sec:failure}, restarted after each declaration, declares twice on the BTOS series, on 10 March 2024 (deceleration) and 6 April 2025 (acceleration). In 400 series simulated from the fitted null (straight line, estimated roughness and scale, BTOS dates and standard errors) it declares at least once in 100 percent and at least twice in 98 percent, 3.1 times on average. The two declarations are what the fitted null produces; they are not evidence of a change in growth.

\paragraph{The universal e-process.} Applied to the 56 waves, the universal e-process peaks at $\log E = 0.39$ ($E=1.5$) on 9 February 2025 against a threshold of $\log 20 = 3.0$ (Figure \ref{fig:btos}). By family, slope alternatives peak at $-1.03$ and level alternatives at $1.07$, belonging to the one-wave fall of 10 March 2024. The result is not sensitive to the prior (Table \ref{tab:app}). In the seven employment-size classes the roughness estimates range from 0.06 to 0.94 and no class exceeds $\log E=-0.6$; the average of the seven e-processes, valid under any dependence, peaks at $-0.95$. In a second series, the share of firms planning to use artificial intelligence, the roughness is $\hat p = 0.080$ (likelihood-ratio statistic 104.7) and the universal e-process peaks at $-0.97$.

\paragraph{A positive control.} The method must be able to detect a real discontinuity. When the change of questionnaire wording on 17 November 2025 is treated as ordinary data, with no regime level, the universal e-process reaches $\log E = 17.2$ ($E \approx 2\times10^{7}$) on 30 November 2025. The monitor is therefore not insensitive; it simply does not declare changes that a rough level explains.

\paragraph{What the series could have revealed.} We inject a change at wave 25 into series simulated from the fitted null on the full 56-wave design and record how often the universal e-process crosses 20 (300 replications per size; Figure \ref{fig:mdc}). A slope change is detected with probability 50 percent at 0.43 points per wave and 80 percent at 0.51; a level shift at 1.75 and 2.12 points. The information scale of Proposition \ref{prop:cost}, $b_k\approx 205$ for a slope change (computed at $\kappa^2=1$; about 160 on the fitted scale), gives $\sqrt{2\cdot 13/b_k}$ of 0.36 to 0.40, which is within 20 percent of the simulated threshold and shows that the information scale is a guide and not a substitute for the calibration. Without roughness the information is 170 times larger. The largest change in the series, the one-wave fall of 1.1 points in March 2024, is below the minimum detectable level shift.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the paper shows.} Anytime-valid monitoring of a trend is only as good as its null. If the latent level is rough, independence-based e-processes are not slightly anticonservative; they are wrong, by a margin that grows with the series, and the failure begins at roughness well below the sampling variance. A null class that includes the roughness restores validity at a price that we decompose and calibrate. In the application, the price is that the series cannot reveal a change smaller than three to four times its average growth, so the absence of declarations is not evidence of constant growth.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The null class is Gaussian; the validity theorem covers any consistent parametric Gaussian covariance class but not non-Gaussian errors, and the failure theorem is for the benchmark monitor in an equally spaced design with a random-walk level. The universal construction is conservative, with realized false-declaration probabilities below 0.001; the calibrated alternative of Table \ref{tab:glr} does not do better in this design but need not be exactly comparable elsewhere. The alternative mixture, the horizon and the grids are fixed in advance, and the application is retrospective. The standard errors enter as fixed weights, panel rotation is not modelled, and the roughness is not identified against correlated sampling error. We analyze two series from one survey; the roughness estimates in the size classes range from 0.06 to 0.94, and a replication in another survey is needed before the magnitude of the roughness is taken as general. We do not claim optimality: the cost decomposition measures the loss but no lower bound on it is proved. A tolerance for small departures from linearity, an exact-scale invariant version and a regret analysis are open.

\paragraph{Implications.} A declaration from a monitor that assumes independent noise should be checked by estimating the roughness and simulating the monitor under the fitted null, as we did. For agencies, the information $b_k$ and the spike-in curve are design quantities that say how large a change a series of a given length, precision and roughness can reveal, and they can be reported with the series.

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:validity}.} Fix $\theta\in\Theta_0$ and $c$. Let $\mathcal{G}_n$ be the $\sigma$-field generated by $R_n$. Since the column space of $X_{n+1}$ restricted to the first $n$ rows is that of $X_n$ (a column for a new regime is zero on earlier rows), $R_n$ is a function of $R_{n+1}$ and $(\mathcal{G}_n)$ is a filtration. Under $\mathcal{N}(Xc,\Sigma_\theta)$ the law of $R_n$ is $\mathcal{N}(0,M_n\Sigma_\theta M_n^\top)$ on the residual space, with density $P_\theta(R_n)$ with respect to a common reference measure. Each component of $Q_n$ is a Gaussian density with positive-definite covariance, so $Q_n\ll P_\theta$ on $\mathcal{G}_n$ and $\Lambda_n=Q_n(R_n)/P_\theta(R_n)$. By consistency of the covariances and of the alternative columns, $Q_{n+1}$ restricted to $\mathcal{G}_n$ is $Q_n$, and likewise $P_\theta$; hence $\Lambda_n$ is the Radon--Nikodym derivative of $Q$ with respect to $P_\theta$ on $\mathcal{G}_n$, a nonnegative $P_\theta$-martingale with $\Lambda_0=1$. Since $\theta\in\Theta_0$, $\sup_{\theta'}P_{\theta'}(R_n)\ge P_\theta(R_n)$, so $0\le E_n\le\Lambda_n$. By Ville's inequality $\Prob_\theta(\sup_n\Lambda_n\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$, and $\{\exists n:E_n\ge1/\alpha\}$ is contained in that event. The Corollary follows because each listed covariance family is consistent in $n$, and a union of classes is a larger $\Theta_0$. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:cost}.} $E_n = Q_n/\sup_\theta P_\theta \ge w_cQ_c/\sup_\theta P_\theta = w_c\,\Lambda_{c,n}\,P_g/\sup_\theta P_\theta$. Take logarithms. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:failure}.} Write $S_i=\sum_{j\ge i}a_j$. Since $a\perp(1,j)$ the linear trend and $L_1$ do not enter $a^\top y=a^\top L+a^\top e$, and with $L_j=L_1+\sum_{i=2}^j\eta_i$ we have $a^\top L=\sum_{i=2}^nS_i\eta_i$. By independence, $\mathrm{Var}(a^\top y)=p\sum_{i\ge2}S_i^2+\sigma^2\|a\|^2$, and $\sum_{i\ge2}S_i^2=a^\top Wa$ up to terms that vanish when $a\perp1$. Hence $\mathrm{Var}(Z_a)=1+(p/\sigma^2)\,a^\top Wa/\|a\|^2$. Since $\psi$ is piecewise continuous, the residual $a_j=\tilde\psi(j/n)+O(n^{-1})$ uniformly off the $O(1)$ discontinuities, so $S_i=n\,\Psi(i/n)+O(1)$ and $\sum_iS_i^2=n^3\int_0^1\Psi^2\,(1+o(1))$, while $\|a\|^2=n\int_0^1\tilde\psi^2\,(1+o(1))$. This gives the stated form. The constant $c(\psi)$ is positive: $\int\tilde\psi^2>0$ because $\psi$ is not linear, and $\Psi\equiv0$ would force $\tilde\psi=0$ almost everywhere. For $\psi=x^2$, $\tilde\psi=(x-\tfrac12)^2-\tfrac1{12}$, $\int\tilde\psi^2=1/180$ and $\Psi(x)=\int_x^1\tilde\psi$ has $\int\Psi^2=1/7560$, so $c=180/7560=1/42$.

For the e-process, $Z_a$ is Gaussian with mean zero and variance $1+\lambda_n\to\infty$, so $\Prob(|Z_a|>z_n)\to1$ for any $z_n=o(\sqrt{\lambda_n})$. With $A^2/B=Z_a^2$, $\log E_n(a)=-\tfrac12\log(1+\tau^2B)+\tfrac12 Z_a^2\,\tau^2B/(1+\tau^2B)$ and $B=\|a\|^2/\sigma^2=O(n)$, which exceeds $\log(2/\alpha)$ once $Z_a^2\ge 2\log(2/\alpha)+\log(1+\tau^2B)+O(1)=O(\log n)$; this has probability tending to one since $Z_a^2$ is of order $n^2$. The half-normal mixture is bounded below by half the normal-mixture value times a constant factor, and the argument is unchanged. $\square$

\paragraph{Sketch for Proposition \ref{prop:scale}.} Let $\mathrm{RSS}^{(0)}_n$ be the residual sum of squares of $y$ on $(1,j)$ and $\rho_n=(a^\top y)^2/(\|a\|^2\,\mathrm{RSS}^{(0)}_n)$ with $a$ the curvature contrast. The profile likelihood ratio of the quadratic model against the linear one is $(1-\rho_n)^{-\nu/2}$ with $\nu=n-2$, and a Laplace approximation of the mixture over the curvature gives $\log E^\sigma_n=\tfrac\nu2\log\{1/(1-\rho_n)\}+O_P(\log n)$. By Donsker's theorem, $(np)^{-1/2}L_{\lfloor nx\rfloor}$ converges to a Brownian motion, and $\rho_n$ converges in distribution to the squared cosine between the detrended Brownian path and the quadratic, which is positive almost surely. On the event $\rho_n>\varepsilon$, whose probability tends to $\Prob(\rho_\infty>\varepsilon)$, $\log E^\sigma_n$ is of order $n$; letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives the claim. We present this as a sketch; the full argument requires uniform control of the Laplace remainder.

\paragraph{Computation.} The universal e-process is computed on the log scale. For each wave $n$ the matrices $P_{1,r}=\Sigma_1^{-1}-\Sigma_1^{-1}X(X^\top\Sigma_1^{-1}X)^{-1}X^\top\Sigma_1^{-1}$, $\Sigma_1=D+rW$, are precomputed for the roughness grid, and the quadratic forms $y^\top P_{1,r}y$ and $z^\top P_{1,r}y$ give $E_n$ in closed form.
